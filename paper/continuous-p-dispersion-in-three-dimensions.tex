\begin{abstract}
  The Continuous $p$-Dispersion Problem with boundary constraints asks
  for the placement of $p$ points in a compact subset of Euclidean space so that
  the minimum distance between any two points, and between the points and the
  boundary of the set, is maximized. It arises in facility placement, network
  design, sampling and particle simulation, is NP-hard, and existing algorithms
  address mainly two-dimensional containers. We introduce an almost-everywhere
  differentiable model and a global optimization algorithm for this problem in
  convex and non-convex three-dimensional polyhedra under any norm-induced
  metric. It extends planar dispersion techniques to three dimensions through
  face orientation, exact metric distances from a point to the faces, edges and
  vertices of the boundary, and an exact signed-tetrahedra point-in-polyhedron test. For
  any polyhedral norm these distances and their gradients follow in closed form
  from the vertices of the dual unit ball, so a metric enters as a finite set of
  vectors. Our solutions match the analytical optima and best-known values in
  the unit cube, attain the optimum wherever it can be certified, and improve on
  the published benchmark for the regular tetrahedron. Given the same wall time
  as the general-purpose solvers IPOPT and SLSQP, it finds a larger radius in 30
  and 23 of 45 test cases respectively, by up to a factor of $2.4$, and a
  smaller one in none. In the eight polyhedral domains of Stoyan
  and Yaskov (2013), it packs more spheres of radius one than published in seven, by up
  to eight, and as many in the eighth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:Intro}

In 1987, \citet{kuby1987} proposed the $p$-Dispersion Problem, asking
to locate $p$ facilities on a network so that the minimum separation
distance between any pair of open facilities is maximized. Later in
1995, \citet{drezner1995} formalized the $p$-Dispersion Problem,
asking for the placement of $p$ points from a subset of $\R^n$ such
that the minimum distance between pairs of points is maximized.
Subsequently, the Continuous $p$-Dispersion Problem (CpDP) with
boundary constraints adds the requirement that a dispersion point
must be sufficiently far away from the boundary of the subset of
$\R^n$, also often referred to as a container. The $p$-Dispersion
Problem is interesting because it formalizes the inherent trade-off
between coverage and separation, to find how a limited number of
points can be optimally distributed over a domain. The $p$-Dispersion
problem is provably NP-complete for the general container
\citep{baurfekete2001} in the number of dispersion points $p$.

Existing work solving the $p$-Dispersion problem primarily focuses on
low-dimensional containers with special geometric properties. For
instance, repulsion-based heuristics have been applied to circular
containers \citep{graham1998} and non-convex two-dimensional polygons
\citep{dai2021}. Other approaches include a 2/3-approximation
algorithm for rectilinear polygons \citep{baurfekete2001} and a Tabu
Search Global Optimization (TSGO) algorithm for general planar
containers \citep{lai2024}. In higher dimensions, work is severely
limited: the nonlinear model of \citet{drezner1995} is formulated in a way
that extends to $\R^n$ but is demonstrated only in the unit square, while
\citet{kazakov2018} developed a grid-based
sphere-packing algorithm that struggles with complex, non-convex
three-dimensional boundaries. \citet{stoyan2013} pack congruent spheres into
polyhedral domains with holes, the decision version of the Euclidean problem:
how many spheres of a given radius fit.

Compared to previous work, the goal of this paper is to approximate
elements of the solution set for the CpDP within general polyhedral
containers with respect to any norm-induced metric in $\mathbb{R}^3$
\cite{lai2024}. The TSGO algorithm proposed by \citet{lai2024} is
difficult to adapt to higher dimensions because it is built around
two-dimensional logic. Their model relies on measuring distances to
the edges of a polygon and uses repeated point-in-polygon tests to
check for constraint violations. We extend this approach to solve the
CpDP for general polyhedral containers in three dimensions. Our
method uses an exact signed-tetrahedra test for point-in-container testing
and replaces their edge-based distance checks with a formulation that
calculates the distance to the faces, edges, and vertices of the
three-dimensional boundary. Those distances are exact in any
norm-induced metric, not only the Euclidean one: as developed in
Section~\ref{sec:polyhedral}, point-to-segment distance is a
one-dimensional convex problem for every norm, and for a polyhedral norm
both it and the point-to-plane distance reduce to finite evaluations over
the vertices of the dual unit ball, which also supply the gradients the
solver needs.

Solving the CpDP in three dimensions reflects how spatial dispersion
is performed in real-world situations where objects have volume and
operate in bounded physical space, such as collision avoidance for
autonomous agents \cite{abdulghafoor2021} or transmitter placement in
complex indoor environments \cite{loganathan2024}. Importantly, the
mathematical abstractions based on \citep{lai2024} established in
this work are designed to be extensible to general $n$-dimensional
containers. While the primary challenge in such a transition lies in
the geometric modeling of arbitrary high-dimensional polyhedra,
containers with uniform constraints, such as an $n$-dimensional
hypercube with limits restricted between 0 and 1, remain
straightforward to model within this framework. We build on the work
from \citet{lai2024} by defining a feasibility-residual function to
approximately solve the CpDP with boundary constraints for convex and
non-convex polyhedral containers in $\R^3$. A feasibility-residual
function satisfies $E(x) = 0$ if and only if $x$ is a feasible solution, and
$E(x) > 0$ otherwise. To minimize this function, we utilize the Tabu
Search heuristic \cite{glover1997} which improves upon standard local
searches by marking previously visited configurations as ``tabu'' to
prevent cycling and encourage the exploration of more optimal regions
of the solution space \cite{lai2024}.

We also generalize the Continuous $p$-Dispersion Problem by allowing
the distance between points and the boundary to be measured using a
norm-induced metric. This allows the algorithm to handle separation constraints
defined by general shapes (like cubes or octahedra) rather than being
limited to standard spherical constraints. This change broadens the
types of dispersion problems the algorithm can solve and provides a
single framework for optimizing point layouts under different
geometric requirements. We validate our algorithm's accuracy by
comparing its results against optimal results in the unit cube proven
by \citet{schaer1966, schaer1994} and the best-known values of Packomania
\citep{packomania}, measuring the relative deterioration to show
how closely our algorithm approaches global maxima. For
non-rectangular containers, we benchmark our results against the
best-known packings for the unit tetrahedron provided by
\citet{kazakov2018}, against optima certified with Gurobi, and against the
sphere counts \citet{stoyan2013} report for their eight domains with holes,
seven of which we exceed. We compare with the general-purpose solvers IPOPT
and SLSQP at equal wall time, and report results for three non-convex polyhedra
of our own design, which have no published values: there the general-purpose
solvers fall furthest behind, and our radii serve as reference values for later
work. We also provide visualizations for our best-achieved configurations in
several containers. The centers of every configuration we
report are published with a standalone script that checks them.

\textbf{Basic Notation:} We use the following standard notation
throughout this paper. Let $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ denote a metric on
$\R^n$ following the usual definition \citep{rosen2012}. The distance
between an element $x \in \R^3$ and a nonempty subset $X \subseteq
\R^3$ is given by $d(x, X) = \inf \set{d(x, y) : y \in X}$. The
\textit{open ball} and \textit{closed ball} of radius $r$ centered at
$x$ are defined as $B_r^o(x) = \set{y \in \R^3 : d(x, y) < r}$ and
$B_r^c(x) = \set{y \in \R^3 : d(x, y) \le r}$, respectively. For any
positive integer $n$, let $[n] = \set{1, \dots, n}$. Finally, for a
subset $X \subseteq \R^3$, $\partial X$ denotes the boundary of $X$,
$X^c = \R^3 \setminus X$ is the complement of $X$, and $X^o = X
\setminus \partial X$ is the interior of $X$.

\section{Problem Formulation}

The Continuous $p$-Dispersion Problem (CpDP) asks to place $p$
points, i.e., \textit{sites}, within a compact subset, i.e., a
\textit{container}, in $\R^n$ so that the minimum pairwise distance
between the sites is maximized \citep{drezner1995}. A variation on
the CpDP includes ``boundary constraints'', which ask that the
minimum distance between any two points, as well as the points and
the boundary of this compact set be maximized \citep{baurfekete2001}.
Most existing algorithms that solve the CpDP do so in the setting of
bounded, polygonal containers in two dimensions, possibly with holes.
In three dimensions, the natural extension is to consider bounded
containers which are polyhedra with polyhedral holes. A
\textit{polyhedron} is a closed and bounded solid $C$ whose boundary
$\partial C$ can be covered by finitely many planes \cite{berg2008}.
A \textit{polyhedral container} is a polyhedron $C$ with holes $U_1,
\dots, U_k \subseteq C$ which are also polyhedra.

We denote $C = (V, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{F})$ where $V$ is the set of vertices
of $C$, $\mathcal{E}$ is the set of edges of $C$ and $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of
faces of $C$; the script letters distinguish these sets from the energy $E$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:E} and from an individual face $F$. We
require that the interior of a polyhedral container be nonempty,
therefore providing the polyhedral container with a consistent
orientation with inward normals \citep{munkres1991}. We write $x \in
C$ for an element $x \in \R^3$ inside the polyhedron but outside its
holes. An element $x \notin C$ is located outside the polyhedron or
inside the polyhedral container's holes.

We now state the Continuous $p$-Dispersion Problem (CpDP)
\citep{drezner1995} with boundary constraints for polyhedral containers with
respect to a metric $d$ on $\R^3$ induced by a norm (i.e., $d(x, y) =
\norm{x - y}$). The metrics of interest ($\ell_1$, $\ell_2$ and
$\ell_\infty$) are all of this form, so this costs no generality in practice.
Expressing $d$ through a norm supplies two useful properties:
\emph{translation invariance}, $d(x + z, y + z) = d(x, y)$, and
\emph{absolute homogeneity}, $\norm{\lambda v} = \abs{\lambda} \norm{v}$ for
all $\lambda \in \R$. Homogeneity is what renders the norm comparable to the
Euclidean metric, and allows for the almost-everywhere differentiability
argument; the metric $d(x, y) = \sqrt{\norm{x - y}_2}$ induces the
usual topology
on $\R^3$ but is not homogeneous. \\

\textbf{Problem:} Let $p \in \N$ and $C \subseteq \R^3$ be a
polyhedral container. We solve the nonlinear optimization problem:
\begin{align*}
  \max_{c_1, \dots, c_p} \quad & D \\
  \text{subject to} \quad & c_1, \dots, c_p \in C, & (i) \\
  & d(c_i, c_j) \ge 2D \text{ for all distinct } i, j \in [p], & (ii) \\
  & d(c_i, \partial C) \ge D \text{ for all } i \in [p]. & (iii)
\end{align*}
Note that we will interpret the value of $D$ to be the radius of the
ball centered at each dispersion point $c_i$. For a feasible solution
$(c_1, \dots, c_p, D) \in (\R^3)^p \times \R$ to the nonlinear
optimization problem, we refer to the elements $c_i \in \R^3$ as
\textit{dispersion points} and $D \in \R_{> 0}$ as the
\textit{radius} of the solution. A \textit{configuration} (of
dispersion points) is the tuple $X = (c_1, \dots, c_p) \in
(\R^3)^p$ of a feasible solution; the ordering is immaterial to the
problem but fixes the indexing used by the sums below.

These constraints enforce three geometric requirements: ($i$) the
\textit{containment constraint} ensures each point $c_i$ lies within
the container; ($ii$) the \textit{dispersion constraint} requires
mutual separation, meaning $B_D^o(c_i) \cap B_D^o(c_j) = \emptyset$
for all distinct $i, j \in [p]$; and ($iii$) the \textit{boundary
constraint} keeps points clear of the boundary, ensuring $B_D^o(c_i)
\cap \partial C = \emptyset$ for all $i \in [p]$.

From the assumption that the interior of the container $C$ is
nontrivial, a feasible solution $(c_1, \dots, c_p, D)$ always exists
\citep{dai2021}.

The CpDP is hard to attack naively because the feasible set of the
nonlinear program is non-convex and admits many local optima. As a
result, standard nonlinear optimizers often get caught in local minima.
Additionally, the number of distance constraints to compute is
$\binom{p}2 = \mathcal{O}(p^2)$, and with minimum distance bounds, it
is highly likely that a random initial configuration will violate the
dispersion constraint (\textit{ii}).

\section{Optimization Model}

In this section we introduce a unified feasibility-residual function
used to solve the CpDP with boundary constraints by modeling
constraint violation in two parts: \textit{dispersion violation} and
\textit{container violation}. Dispersion violation stems from
dispersion points being located too close together; namely, when
$d(c_i, c_j) < 2D$ for some fixed $D > 0$, constraint (\textit{ii})
is violated. Container violation is a result of dispersion points
being located too close to the boundary of the container; namely,
when $d(c_i, \partial C) < D$ for some fixed $D > 0$, constraint
(\textit{iii}) is violated.

By combining these two components into a single feasibility-residual
function, we can address inherent geometric complexities associated
with any container. In general, the feasible region for the solution
set to the CpDP is non-convex. Moreover, as the number of dispersion
points $p$ increases, the feasible region will contain many local
optima \citep{lai2024}.

\citet{lai2024} proposed a novel framework for the two-dimensional
CpDP by unifying dispersion and boundary constraints into a single,
non-negative feasibility-residual function, $E_D$. They
conceptualized constraint violations as \textit{energy}, where the
total energy of a configuration corresponds to its distance from
feasibility. For $p$ points in the plane and a fixed
$D \ge 0$, this function is defined as $\displaystyle{E_D =
  \sum_{1 \le i < j \le p} \ell^2_{ij} + \sum_{i = 1}^p O_i}$ where $\ell_{ij}$ is the dispersion penalty between points
$c_i$ and $c_j$, and $O_i$ is the boundary penalty for point $c_i$. A
crucial property of this formulation is that $E_D(X) = 0$ if and only
if $(X, D)$ is a feasible solution. Because $E_D$ is
almost-everywhere differentiable, it allows for the application of
efficient gradient-based optimization methods, such as the
limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno algorithm with bound constraints (L-BFGS-B)
\citep{fletcher1987, byrd1995}. This approach proved exceptionally powerful,
enabling the rapid convergence to local optima from random initial
configurations.

Extending the optimization model from \citet{lai2024} to three
dimensions introduces several significant challenges. While the
dispersion penalty term is easy enough to compute in any finite
number of dimensions, computing the boundary penalty term in three
dimensions becomes computationally complex. First, \citet{lai2024}
define their boundary constraint term in a manner which heavily
relies on planar geometry where counterclockwise traversal of closed
polygonal loops provides a canonical way to distinguish the interior
from the exterior. We generalize boundary orientation of polygonal
containers from two-dimensional vertex traversal to three dimensions
by orienting the faces of the polyhedron using inward normals. By
assigning an inward-pointing normal to each face, we explicitly
define the interior half-space of the polyhedron. In our algorithm,
these normals serve as the directional basis for calculating
distance-to-boundary terms for dispersion points and performing
feasibility checks.

Additionally, point-in-container testing is fundamental to the
boundary constraint term, as it dictates whether to apply a repulsive
force to maintain interiority or an attractive force to recover
feasibility from the exterior. While point-in-polygon testing admits
efficient deterministic routines \citep{wang2005}, in three dimensions we
use the signed-tetrahedra inclusion test of \citet{feito1997};
\citet{ogayar2005} compare point-in-solid strategies more broadly. Each face is
fanned into triangles, oriented so that the triangles of an outer boundary
face out of the container and those of a hole face into it. With a fixed
origin $O$ outside the container, each triangle $(a, b, c)$ spans a
tetrahedron $(O, a, b, c)$ with sign $s = \operatorname{sgn} \det[a - O,\, b -
O,\, c - O]$, and these signed tetrahedra sum to the container as a chain: a
point $x$ is inside exactly when $\sum s \, [x \in (O, a, b, c)] \ne 0$. The
test is exact and deterministic, so $E$ stays a deterministic function of the
configuration, and its cost is linear in the number of triangles. It is
ambiguous only when $x$ lies on the boundary of some tetrahedron, a set of
measure zero. Such a point is counted again from a second origin, and one
still ambiguous, which can happen only on the plane of a face, is decided by
ray casting \citep{horvat2012}. On every container in this paper the test
agrees with a majority vote of eight ray casts on random and near-wall points,
and it is 10 to 120 times faster than that vote.

A point pressed against a wall, the regime the dispersion points occupy at
convergence, is delicate for any inclusion test, so we do not leave it to one:
a point within a tolerance $\epsilon_{\text{plane}}$ of some face is classified
as interior before the test runs. The Active Face
rule of the next section is made inclusive to the same
$\epsilon_{\text{plane}}$, so that a point admitted as interior by this
test cannot simultaneously fail to see the very wall that admitted it.
Were the two tolerances to disagree, a point resting on a wall would lose
that wall from its active set and report a clearance measured to some
distant feature. These geometric tools provide the foundation
needed to extend the underlying optimization model to three dimensions.

With this geometric framework in place, we now adapt the optimization
model presented in \citet{lai2024} to three dimensions. The
\textit{energy of a solution} $(c_1, \dots, c_p, D)$ will be measured
by its quantification of constraint violation. Recall that high
energy solutions have a high degree of constraint violation and low
energy solutions have a low degree of constraint violation.

We first record the regularity facts used to establish that the
feasibility-residual function is almost-everywhere differentiable. Both penalty
terms below are built from metric distances composed with the same
one-dimensional map, so it is convenient to isolate their common structure.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lipschitz}
  Let $d$ be induced by a norm on $\R^3$. Then
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)] $(x, y) \mapsto d(x, y)$ is Lipschitz on $\R^3 \times \R^3$ with
      respect to the Euclidean structure;
    \item[(b)] for any nonempty $S \subseteq \R^3$, $x \mapsto d(x, S)$ is
      $1$-Lipschitz;
    \item[(c)] for nonempty $S_1, \dots, S_m \subseteq \R^3$, the pointwise
      minimum $x \mapsto \min_{k \in [m]} d(x, S_k)$ is $1$-Lipschitz;
    \item[(d)] $g(t) = \paren{\max \set{0, t}}^2$ belongs to $C^1(\R)$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Consequently, if $f$ is locally Lipschitz then $g \circ f$ is differentiable
  almost everywhere.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  (a) The triangle inequality gives $\abs{d(x, y) - d(x', y')} \le d(x, x') +
  d(y, y')$. Since all norms on $\R^3$ are equivalent, there is $C > 0$ with
  $d(u, v) \le C \norm{u - v}_2$, so $d$ is Lipschitz with respect to the
  Euclidean structure. (b) For every $s \in S$, $d(x, S) \le d(x, y) + d(y, s)$;
  taking the infimum over $s$ and exchanging $x$ and $y$ yields $\abs{d(x, S) -
  d(y, S)} \le d(x, y)$. (c) A minimum of finitely many $1$-Lipschitz functions
  is $1$-Lipschitz. (d) $g'(t) = 2 \max \set{0, t}$ is continuous, so squaring
  removes the kink that $\max \set{0, t}$ has at the origin. For the final
  claim, $f$ is differentiable almost everywhere by Rademacher's Theorem
  \citep{evans2015}, and the chain rule applies wherever $f$ is differentiable, since $g$ is
  continuously differentiable.
\end{proof}

First, we define the penalty term for dispersion violation. Let
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{i, j} = \max \set{0, 2D - d(c_i, c_j)},
  \label{eq:lij}
\end{equation}
hence we get $\ell_{i, j} = 0$ if and only if $d(c_i, c_j) \ge 2D$.
Therefore the total pairwise dispersion penalty
$\displaystyle{\sum_{1 \le i < j \le p} \ell_{i, j}^2}$ equals $0$ if and only if all the dispersion points are
sufficiently far from each other. Moreover $\ell_{i, j}^2 = g \paren{2D -
d(c_i, c_j)}$ with $g$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:lipschitz}(d), and $(c_i, c_j)
\mapsto 2D - d(c_i, c_j)$ is Lipschitz by Lemma~\ref{lem:lipschitz}(a). Hence
$\ell_{i, j}^2$ is differentiable almost everywhere for all $i, j$.

\subsection{Distances under a polyhedral norm}
\label{sec:polyhedral}

The boundary penalty measures the distance from a dispersion point to three
kinds of feature: the container's vertices, its edges, and its faces. Only the
first is immediate. We record here the construction used for the other two,
because it is what makes the model metric-agnostic: a norm enters the algorithm
only through a finite set of vectors, and one argument then settles the
distance, the point realizing it, and the gradient, for segments and planes
alike.

Throughout, $\norm{\cdot}$ is a norm on $\R^3$, $d(x, y) = \norm{x - y}$, and
$\norm{z}_* = \max \set{\ang{z, u} : \norm{u} \le 1}$ is the dual norm. Call
$\norm{\cdot}$ \emph{polyhedral} when its unit ball is a polytope; equivalently,
when the dual unit ball is a polytope with vertices $g_1, \dots, g_K$, so that
\begin{equation}
  \norm{z} = \max_{k \in [K]} \ang{g_k, z}.
  \label{eq:gen}
\end{equation}
Both non-Euclidean metrics used in this paper are of this kind. For
$\ell_\infty$ the dual ball is the $\ell_1$ ball, whose vertices are the six
vectors $\pm e_i$; for $\ell_1$ the dual ball is the $\ell_\infty$ ball, whose
vertices are the eight sign vectors $(\pm 1, \pm 1, \pm 1)$. A further
polyhedral norm (weighted, anisotropic, hexagonal) is supplied to the
algorithm by giving its generators, not by deriving new geometry.

\textbf{Segments.} For a segment $[a, b]$ and a point $x$,
\begin{equation}
  d(x, [a, b]) = \min_{t \in [0, 1]} \norm{(x - a) - t (b - a)}.
  \label{eq:segdist}
\end{equation}
The function being minimized is a norm composed with an affine map of $t$,
hence convex on $[0, 1]$ \emph{for every norm}. Point-to-segment distance is
therefore always a one-dimensional convex problem, never a per-metric
derivation; what a metric changes is only how the minimum is located.
Substituting Equation~\ref{eq:gen} into Equation~\ref{eq:segdist} gives
\begin{align*}
  d(t) &= \max_{k \in [K]} \paren{\alpha_k + t \beta_k}, \\
  \alpha_k &= \ang{g_k, x - a}, \quad \beta_k = -\ang{g_k, b - a},
\end{align*}
the upper envelope of $K$ affine functions: convex and piecewise linear. The
minimum of such an envelope over $[0, 1]$ is attained at an endpoint or at a
kink, and every kink is a crossing of two of the lines, so the exact minimizer
lies in the finite set
\begin{equation*}
  \paren{\set{0, 1} \cup \set{\tfrac{\alpha_j - \alpha_i}{\beta_i - \beta_j}
  : i < j, \ \beta_i \ne \beta_j}} \cap [0, 1],
\end{equation*}
and is obtained by evaluation rather than by search.

\textbf{Gradients.} Only $\alpha_k$ depends on $x$, with $\partial \alpha_k /
\partial x = g_k$. If the minimum is attained at an endpoint, $t^*$ is pinned
there under small perturbations of $x$, and Danskin's Theorem gives $\partial d
/ \partial x = g_{k^*}$ for any $k^*$ attaining the maximum. If instead it is
attained at a crossing of lines $i$ and $j$, then $t^* = (\alpha_j -
\alpha_i)/(\beta_i - \beta_j)$ moves with $x$, and differentiating $d =
\alpha_i + t^* \beta_i$ through that dependence yields
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\partial d}{\partial x}
  = g_i + \beta_i \, \frac{g_j - g_i}{\beta_i - \beta_j}.
\end{equation*}
The two cases are the endpoint and interior branches of one expression; neither
is specific to $\ell_1$ or $\ell_\infty$.

\textbf{Planes.} Let $P = \set{y : \ang{N, y - q} = 0}$ and write $h = \ang{N,
x - q}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  d(x, P) = \frac{\abs{h}}{\norm{N}_*},
  \label{eq:plane}
\end{equation}
and the minimum is attained at $x - s u$, where $s = h / \norm{N}_*$ and $u$ is
any vector with $\norm{u} \le 1$ and $\ang{N, u} = \norm{N}_*$. Indeed, for
every $y \in P$ we have $\ang{N, x - y} = h$, so $\abs{h} \le \norm{N}_*
\norm{x - y}$ by definition of the dual norm, giving $d(x, P) \ge \abs{h} /
\norm{N}_*$; and the stated point lies in $P$ and realizes the bound. For a
polyhedral norm the maximum defining $\norm{N}_*$ is attained at a vertex of
the unit ball, so $u$ is found among finitely many candidates. For $\ell_2$,
$\norm{N}_* = \norm{N}_2$ and $u = N / \norm{N}_2$ is unique, and
Equation~\ref{eq:plane} reduces to orthogonal projection.

Two features of Equation~\ref{eq:plane} deserve emphasis, because the
Euclidean intuition fails on both. First, the orthogonal projection of $x$ onto
$P$ lies at distance $\abs{h} \norm{N} / \norm{N}_2^2$ from $x$. Since
$\ang{N, N} \le \norm{N} \, \norm{N}_*$, this is never less than the distance of
Equation~\ref{eq:plane}, and it equals it only when $\norm{N}_2^2 = \norm{N} \,
\norm{N}_*$. Under $\ell_1$ or $\ell_\infty$ this holds exactly when all
nonzero components of $N$ have the same absolute value, as for the
axis-aligned walls of the cube. Using the projection in place of
Equation~\ref{eq:plane} therefore over-estimates the distance from a
dispersion point to any wall where equality fails. Second,
the nearest point need not be unique: the set of optimal directions
\begin{equation*}
  U^* = \set{u : \norm{u} \le 1, \ \ang{N, u} = \norm{N}_*}
\end{equation*}
is a face of the unit ball, and reduces to a single direction only when that
face is a vertex. For $\ell_\infty$ with $N = e_1$, every $u$ with $u_1 = 1$
and $\norm{u}_\infty \le 1$ is optimal, so the nearest points of $P$ fill a
square rather than forming a single foot. All of them lie at the same distance
from $x$, so Equation~\ref{eq:plane} is unambiguous; but they lie at different
places on $P$, and the next definition turns on \emph{where} they lie.

Before we define the boundary constraint term, we need two
auxiliary definitions which are three-dimensional analogues of the
definitions presented by \citet{lai2024}:

\textbf{Active Face:} Consider a polyhedral container $C$ with a face
$F$ and a point $c$. Orient the normal vector $N_F$ of face $F$
inwards and denote $q_F \in F$ as the point in which $N_F$ originates
from. \textit{A face $F$ is active with respect to $c$} if either $c
\in C$ and $N_F \cdot (c - q_F) \ge 0$ or if $c \notin C$ and $N_F
\cdot (c - q_F) < 0$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:active_planes_inside} depicts the active faces for a

Fig.~\ref{fig:active_planes_outside} depicts the active faces for a

of as an oriented plane that is geometrically relevant to the point
$c$. If $c$ lies inside the polyhedron, then only the faces whose
inward-normals minimally constrain how close $c$ is to the boundary
of the container constitute the set of active faces. Alternatively,
if $c$ lies outside the boundary of the polyhedron, an active face is
blocking $c$ from entering the polyhedron. Active faces will
contribute to the boundary constraint in the feasibility-residual function.

\textbf{Active Footpoint:} Consider a dispersion point $c$ and an
active face $F$ of the container $C$, and let $P$ be the plane through
$q_F$ with normal $N_F$. A \textit{foot} of $c$ on $P$ is a nearest point
of $P$ to $c$ in the metric $d$; by Equation~\ref{eq:plane} every foot
lies at distance $\abs{\ang{N_F, c - q_F}} / \norm{N_F}_*$ from $c$. We
say $c$ has an \textit{active footpoint} $h$ with respect to $F$ if some
foot of $c$ on $P$ lies on $F$, and we take $h$ to be such a foot. We
denote $H^i$ to be the set of all active footpoints with respect to the
dispersion point $c_i$.

Under the Euclidean metric the foot is unique and equals the orthogonal
projection of $c$ onto $P$, which recovers the construction of
\citet{lai2024}. Under a general norm it is neither unique nor orthogonal,
and the existential quantifier in the definition is load-bearing. Since all
feet of $c$ on $P$ are equidistant from $c$, the \emph{distance} an active
footpoint contributes does not depend on which foot is named; what does
depend on it is whether the named point lies on $F$, and hence whether $F$
contributes to the penalty at all. Requiring only that \emph{some} foot lie
on $F$ makes this a question about the geometry of $P \cap F$ rather than
about an arbitrary selection, and it is exactly the property
Lemma~\ref{lem:zero} needs.

Fig.~\ref{fig:footpoints_outside} depicts a case where not all
footpoints of a dispersion point are active. The active footpoint $h$
of a dispersion point $c$ allows us to measure distances between
dispersion points and the minimally relevant planes of the container,
thereby providing a reference for the distance between a dispersion
point and the container's boundary. When a nearest point of $P$ falls
directly on the face $F$, $F$ is the boundary face that governs how
close $c$ may move toward the container’s boundary, and the
point-to-face distance is determined by the separation between $c$ and
this footpoint. When no nearest point of $P$ lies on $F$, the nearest
point of $F$ itself lies on the relative boundary of $F$, which is a
union of edges; the penalty then reaches that part of the boundary
through $\mathcal{E}$ rather than through $H^i$, and nothing is lost.

Orientation equips the polyhedron with coherent inward normals,
making the active face selection rule an orientation-consistent
generalization of the counterclockwise active-edge rule used in
the planar formulation \citep{lai2024}. Unlike the two-dimensional
case, where the boundary is a closed, non-self-intersecting loop of
segments with an inherent cyclic ordering, the boundary of a
three-dimensional container forms a polyhedral complex with no
analogous ordering. The extension from active edges \citep{lai2024}
to active faces and their associated footpoints provides the
geometric machinery needed to generalize boundary interactions from
vertices and edges in two dimensions to vertices, edges, and faces in
three dimensions. Thus, active faces constitute the minimal and
geometrically natural extension of the active-edge concept from two
to three dimensions, enabling a unified treatment of boundary
constraints in arbitrary polyhedral domains.

Now, we define the boundary penalty for a dispersion point $c_i$ as
\begin{equation}
  O_i =
  \begin{cases}
    \paren{D + 2 \min \set{d(c_i, v) : v \in V \cup H^i}}^2 & c_i
    \notin C, \\[4pt]
    \displaystyle\sum_{v \, \in \, V \, \cup \, \mathcal{E} \, \cup \, H^i}
    \paren{\max \set{0,\ D - d(c_i, v)}}^2 & c_i \in C.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:O_i}
\end{equation}
When $c_i \in C$, the penalty accumulates the squared \emph{shortfall} of each
boundary feature's distance below $D$: it vanishes exactly when the open ball
$B^o_D(c_i)$ meets no vertex, edge or active footpoint of the container, and
otherwise grows with the depth of the violation. If the distance from $c_i$ to a
vertex, edge or footpoint exceeds $D$, that feature contributes nothing.

The exterior branch minimizes over $V \cup H^i$ only. Omitting $\mathcal{E}$
costs nothing there: outside the container the penalty has no zero to reach, so
its role is not to measure the distance to the boundary exactly but to be
positive and to point back inside, and $V \cup H^i$ already determines a
direction of return. Inside, by contrast, the edges are indispensable: they
are what covers the case in which no foot of $c_i$ lands on its face, as the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:zero} shows.

When $c_i \notin C$, a penalty is imposed regardless of $D$, since the point
violates the containment constraint (\textit{i}). The squared form is bounded
below by $D^2 > 0$ and has non-vanishing derivative in the distance from $c_i$
to the container, so an exterior point is not merely penalized but actively
driven back toward it; a penalty differing from the interior branch by an
additive constant would leave the gradient unchanged and exert no such force.

We now define the energy function $E: (\R^3)^p \times \R \to \R$
where $D > 0$ is fixed and $X = (c_1, \dots, c_p) \in (\R^3)^p$ by
\begin{equation}
  E(X, D) = \sum_{1 \le i < j \le p} \ell_{i, j}^2 + \sum_{i = 1}^p O_i.
  \label{eq:E}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:zero}
  \textit{For fixed $D > 0$, $O_i = 0$ if and
    only if $c_i \in C$ and $d(c_i, \partial C) \ge D$. Consequently
    $E(X, D) = 0$
  if and only if $(X, D)$ is feasible.}

  \textit{Proof.} If $c_i \notin C$ then $O_i \ge D^2 > 0$. Suppose $c_i \in
  C$. Every term of the interior branch vanishes exactly when $d(c_i, v) \ge D$
  for all $v \in V \cup \mathcal{E} \cup H^i$, so it suffices to prove the
  covering identity
  \begin{equation}
    \min_{v \, \in \, V \, \cup \, \mathcal{E} \, \cup \, H^i} d(c_i, v)
    = d(c_i, \partial C).
    \label{eq:covering}
  \end{equation}
  Each feature on the left is a subset of $\partial C$, so the left side is at
  least the right. For the reverse inequality, $\partial C$ is compact, so the
  infimum defining $d(c_i, \partial C)$ is attained at some $y^*$. If $y^*$ lies
  on an edge of $C$, that edge belongs to $\mathcal{E}$ and the left side of
  Equation~\ref{eq:covering} attains $d(c_i, \partial C)$. Otherwise $y^*$ lies
  in the relative interior of a face $F$. We claim $F$ is active. If not, then
  $\ang{N_F, c_i - q_F} < 0$, so $c_i$ lies strictly on the outward side of the
  plane of $F$. Near a point of the relative interior of $F$, $C$ lies on the
  inward side of that plane, so points of the open segment $(y^*, c_i)$ near
  $y^*$ lie outside $C$, while $c_i \in C$. That segment therefore meets
  $\partial C$ at some $z$ strictly between $y^*$ and $c_i$, and $d(c_i, z) <
  d(c_i, y^*)$, contradicting the minimality of $y^*$.

  Since $y^*$ lies in the relative interior of $F$ and minimizes $d(c_i, \cdot)$
  over $F$, it is a local, and by convexity of the norm a global, minimizer of
  $d(c_i, \cdot)$ over the plane of $F$. It is therefore a foot of $c_i$ that
  lies on $F$, so $c_i$ has an active footpoint with respect to $F$. By
  Equation~\ref{eq:plane} that footpoint lies at distance $d(c_i, y^*) = d(c_i,
  \partial C)$, and the left side of Equation~\ref{eq:covering} attains it,
  proving the identity. Hence $O_i = 0$ exactly when $d(c_i, \partial C) \ge D$, which is
  constraint (\textit{iii}).

  The first term of Equation~\ref{eq:E} vanishes precisely when $d(c_i, c_j)
  \ge 2D$ for all $i \ne j$, which is constraint (\textit{ii}). Both terms are
  non-negative, so their sum vanishes only when each term does.
  \hfill $\square$
\end{lemma}

The first term of Equation~\ref{eq:E} deals with the dispersion
constraint (\textit{ii}) of the CpDP, and the second term with the containment
and boundary constraints (\textit{i}) and (\textit{iii}). When $D$ is fixed, we
write $E_D(X) := E(X, D)$.

Throughout, the quantity we report is the \emph{radius} $D$, not the minimum
separation. Constraints (\textit{ii}) and (\textit{iii}) are deliberately
asymmetric in it (two points must be $2D$ apart, while a point must clear
the boundary by $D$) because $D$ is the radius of the largest ball that can
be centered at every dispersion point without overlapping another or leaving
the container. A configuration reported at $D$ therefore has minimum pairwise
distance $2D$. Comparisons against sphere-packing tables
\citep{packomania, schaer1966} are radius to radius.

Equation~\ref{eq:E} gives a feasibility-residual function which lifts
the optimization model proposed by \citet{lai2024} from two
dimensions into three dimensions. It is also differentiable almost everywhere.
Fix a configuration at which no active footpoint lies on the boundary of its
face. On a neighborhood of such a configuration the active sets $H^1, \dots,
H^p$ are constant, so $E$ is a finite sum of terms $g \paren{D - d(\cdot, v)}$
with $v$ ranging over a fixed set; each is locally Lipschitz by
Lemma~\ref{lem:lipschitz}, hence so is $E$, and Rademacher's Theorem applies.
Two families of configurations are excluded: those at which some footpoint
meets the boundary of its face, and those at which some $c_i$ lies on $\partial
C$, where $O_i$ switches branches. Each is cut out by finitely many equations,
so both are finite unions of sets of codimension one and therefore have
Lebesgue measure zero. Hence $E$ is differentiable almost everywhere on
$(\R^3)^p \times \R_{>0}$, which is what permits the use of gradient-based
solvers such as L-BFGS-B \citep{fletcher1987, byrd1995}.

We note that $E$ is not continuous everywhere, and that both of its
discontinuities come from the same source: under a sum, a single geometric
constraint is witnessed by a face, its edges and its vertices at once, and the
sum weights it by a multiplicity that changes as $c_i$ moves. In the interior,
a footpoint leaving its face has its contribution dropped while the
corresponding edge term remains; in the H-box at $D = 0.15$ we measure a step
of about $2 \times 10^{-2}$ across such an event along a path that stays
strictly inside the container. In a convex box under $\ell_2$ no such event
occurs, since every interior point's foot lies on its face. At the wall, the interior branch tends to the sum of
$(D - d_f)^2$ over every feature within $D$, which exceeds the $D^2$ the
exterior branch takes there whenever more than one feature is in range, as it always is near
an edge or a corner. Crossing the cube's floor $0.04$ from an edge, $E$
steps from $0.0467$ to $0.0225$. This
does not affect almost-everywhere differentiability, since the argument above is
local and applies on the open set where the active sets are constant, but
it does mean a line search may encounter a discontinuity.

Aggregating the interior branch as $\paren{\max \set{0, D - \min_v d(c_i,
v)}}^2$ removes both jumps at once (one constraint is then counted once,
and the interior branch tends to $D^2$ at the wall, matching the exterior one)
and makes $E$ globally Lipschitz, at which point
Lemma~\ref{lem:lipschitz}(c) gives almost-everywhere differentiability
directly, with no excluded set. Under the minimum neither measurement above
jumps: across the H-box event its value is unchanged, and at the cube's floor
it reads $0.0225$ on both sides. We retain the sum because, in development
runs on an earlier version of the solver, it performed marginally better at
the top of the distribution: across four
container--$p$ cells the best radius found under the minimum was between
$0.975$ and $1.000$ times that found under the sum. The minimum was, however,
the more reliable of the two per seed, raising the mean radius from $0.359$ to
$0.388$ on the H-box at $p = 10$ and from $0.106$ to $0.112$ on the
tetrahedron at $p = 10$, so the two aggregations trade reliability against
best-case quality rather than dominating one another.

Both $E$ and $\Phi_\mu$ are differentiated in closed form rather than by
finite differences. The dispersion term $\ell_{i,j}^2$ contributes $-2 \max
\set{0, 2D - d(c_i,c_j)} \, \partial d / \partial c_i$ to the gradient at
$c_i$, the negative of that at $c_j$, and $4 \max \set{0, 2D - d(c_i,c_j)}$ to
$\partial E / \partial D$; the vertex and
footpoint terms differentiate through the metric directly, using the
subgradient of the norm at the displacement; the edge terms use the segment
gradient of Section~\ref{sec:polyhedral}; and the face terms use $\partial
\paren{\abs{h} / \norm{N}_*} / \partial c_i = \mathrm{sgn}(h) N /
\norm{N}_*$, which is independent of which foot the face contributes. The
derivative with respect to $D$ accumulates alongside, so one pass returns
$\Phi_\mu$, $\nabla_X \Phi_\mu$ and $\partial \Phi_\mu / \partial D$
together.

This is not merely an optimization. A finite-difference gradient over the
$3p + 1$ variables costs that many extra evaluations of $E$, or twice that
for central differences, against the single pass the closed form needs. On
the unit cube, on one core of the cluster, we measure the analytic gradient as
48 times faster than central differences at $p = 10$ and 323 times faster at
$p = 60$, the gap widening
with $p$ roughly as the count of perturbations predicts. Just as importantly,
a differencing step interacts badly with a function that is only
almost-everywhere differentiable: a step that straddles a kink returns a
slope belonging to neither side, injecting noise that the closed form does
not have.

\section{Global Optimization Algorithm}

In this section we propose our global optimization algorithm which
builds upon the framework introduced by \citet{lai2024}. The global
optimization process is structured as a multi-start pipeline to
navigate the complex, non-convex solution space. In each global
iteration, the algorithm restarts by generating an initial random
configuration of $p$ points uniformly distributed within the
polyhedral container. This multi-start approach is necessary because
the problem’s non-smooth objective function and numerous distance
constraints frequently trap standard optimization tools in poor local
optima \citep{dai2021}. As shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:tabu_search_overview}, within each iteration, the Tabu
Search procedure acts as a feasibility solver by minimizing the
feasibility-residual function of Equation~\ref{eq:E} to find a valid
configuration given a fixed minimum distance $D$. Once a feasible
configuration is found, a distance adjustment procedure applies the
Sequential Unconstrained Minimization Technique (SUMT)
\citep{fiacco1964} to iteratively maximize $D$ while preserving the
feasibility of the configuration.

    demonstrates a two-stage approach: first, Tabu Search is employed
    to relocate a candidate sphere (red) from an initial, infeasible

\subsection{Tabu Search}

We employ a Tabu Search procedure \citep{glover1997}, following the
framework used by \citet{lai2024}. Tabu Search is a combinatorial
heuristic designed to escape poor local configurations (high-energy)
and move the system toward a better (lower-energy) arrangement of
dispersion points inside the container. The core principle behind the
Tabu Search is the \textit{tabu list} which maintains a short-term
list of recent moves that are temporarily forbidden in subsequent
iterations \citep{glover1997}. The tabu list ensures the algorithm
does not fall into cycles of re-evaluating configurations.

A move in our setting relocates a point to a position in $\R^3$, and a
continuous position never recurs exactly, so a list keyed on positions would
forbid nothing. We therefore key an entry on the pair (index of the moved
point, cell of a grid of side $\delta = D/20$ containing its destination),
which makes ``the same move'' a statement about arriving in the same small
region rather than at the same coordinates. Tying $\delta$ to $D$ rather than
to the container keeps the granularity of the memory proportional to the
scale of the configuration being built.

Entries expire two ways. Each is forbidden for a tenure of $7$ iterations,
after which it lapses whether or not the list is full; and the list holds at
most $20$ entries, the least recent being evicted when it overflows. We also
add the \emph{reverse} of each accepted move, which is what prevents the
search from immediately returning a point to the cell it just left. Finally,
the prohibition is subject to an \textit{aspiration criterion}: a forbidden
move is taken anyway if it yields a configuration strictly better than the
best seen so far. Tabu status is a heuristic about where the search has
already been, and it should never be allowed to veto an outright improvement.

The Tabu Search heuristic incentivizes long-term exploration of the
solution space. The maximum number of successive moves the algorithm
is allowed to explore without improving from the initial
configuration is specified by a parameter $\beta$. If Tabu Search
makes $\beta$ iterations without improving on the last-improved
configuration, then the procedure terminates. A small $\beta$ enables
a quicker Tabu Search, but only scans for solutions which are a few
moves different from the initial configurations. A large $\beta$
enables a deeper scan of neighboring solutions, but forces a longer
runtime for Tabu Search. Tabu Search proves to be a powerful tool for
efficiently scanning families of solutions using combinatorial
optimization methods on a continuous region and transforming
high-energy configurations to low-energy configurations.

For a given configuration, we quantify
the \textit{energy of a point} by the degree to which it violates the
dispersion and boundary constraints. We define the energy of a
dispersion point $c_i$ as
\begin{equation}
  E_D(c_i) = \sum_{j \ne i} \ell_{i, j}^2 + O_i
  \label{eq:dispersion_point_energy}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{i, j}$ is the dispersion penalty term and $O_i$ captures
the boundary violation term as in Equation~\ref{eq:O_i}. To propose
new candidate positions for dispersion points, we consider
\textit{vacancy sites}, which are random locations inside the
container not currently occupied by a dispersion point. A vacancy
site $c$ has energy
\begin{equation}
  E_D(c) = \sum_{j = 1}^p \ell_{c, j}^2 + O_{c}
  \label{eq:vacancy_energy}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{c, j}$ replaces $c_i$ with $c$ in Equation~\ref{eq:lij}
and $O_c$ replaces $c_i$ with $c$ in Equation~\ref{eq:O_i}. Vacancy
sites with low energy represent positions that are more compatible
with the current configuration, i.e., points where it would be the
``easiest'' to insert another ball.

A \textit{neighbor} $X_{nb}$ of a solution $X$ can be found by
\textit{moving} a single dispersion point of $X$ to a new location.
The \textit{neighborhood} $N(X)$ is the set of neighbors of $X$. To
select the most promising neighbor of a given configuration $X$, we
sample $N(X)$ by identifying the $Q$ highest-energy dispersion points
and the $Q$ lowest-energy vacancy sites selected from a pool of $10p$
randomly generated candidates, where $p$ is the total number of
dispersion points. While \citet{lai2024} suggest generating only $5p$
vacancy sites, we increased this sample size to $10p$ to maintain
sufficient search breadth. We
analyze the possible moves between these sets to identify the pairing
that offers the best potential reduction in energy.

For a specific dispersion-vacancy move, the resulting configuration
is merely a rough approximation of a feasible solution. Since vacancy
sites are generated randomly, simply placing a point at one of these
coordinates rarely resolves the feasibility-residual value, defined
in Equation~\ref{eq:E}, to zero. Therefore, we do not evaluate the
quality of this raw move directly. Instead, we pass this intermediate
configuration to our local optimization subroutine,
\textsc{LocalOpt}. \textsc{LocalOpt} serves as the mandatory bridge
between the discrete combinatorial search and the continuous solution
space. It utilizes a gradient-based solver, specifically the
limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno algorithm with bound constraints (L-BFGS-B)
\citep{fletcher1987, byrd1995}, to drive the rough candidate configuration
toward the nearest smooth minimum of the energy function.
Consequently, the Tabu Search algorithm never compares unrefined
moves; it strictly compares the \textit{locally optimized} outcomes
of those moves to decide if a step is an improvement.

However, even with this precise local refinement, the global solution
space of Equation~\ref{eq:E} remains non-convex and populated with
numerous local minima \citep{lai2024}. To prevent the algorithm from
becoming trapped in the first deep valley it encounters, we integrate
Monotonic Basin Hopping (\textsc{MBH}) as a heuristic for global
exploration, as suggested by \citet{lai2024}. Unlike Tabu Search,
which evolves a solution along a specific path, \textsc{MBH} samples
the global landscape by ``hopping'' between independent basins (i.e.,
regions converging to a single minimum) \citep{back2013}. It operates
by taking a solution $(X,D)$, performing random perturbations to jump
into a neighboring basin, and then applying \textsc{LocalOpt}. We run 10
such hops per call. Each hop displaces every coordinate by up to $D$, so the
step scales with the problem rather than with the container, and its result
is kept only if the residual does not rise: the walk is monotone, and the step
never worsens the configuration it is given. A walk that also accepts uphill
minima can cross barriers this one cannot. In tests on an earlier version of
the solver, with four seeds at $p = 100$ under $\ell_2$, it reached the best
basin no more often on the cube and less often on the star, so we keep the
monotone walk.

\textsc{MBH} is applied inside the Tabu Search loop rather than as a
separate outer layer, on the configuration that results from taking the
chosen move (line~\ref{line:mbh} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:tabu_search}). The two
heuristics address different failure modes and compose naturally there:
the tabu memory prevents the search from re-taking a move it has just
undone, while \textsc{MBH} perturbs whatever configuration that move
produced. Placing it outside the loop would leave each tabu iteration to
terminate in its own basin with no means of leaving it.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Tabu Search}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $C$ (Container), $X$ (configuration), $D \in \R_{> 0}$,
    $\beta \in \Z_{> 0}$, $Q \in \Z_{> 0}$, $\epsilon \in \R_{> 0}$
    \State $Q \gets \min \set{Q, p}$
    \State $X \gets \textsc{LocalOpt}(X, D)$
    \State $X^{\text{best}} \gets X$, $\emph{NoImprove} \gets 0$,
    $\emph{TabuList} \gets \set{}$
    \While{$\emph{NoImprove} \le \beta$ and $E_D(X^{\text{best}}) > \epsilon$}
    \State $p_1, \dots, p_Q \gets \textsc{HighEnergyPoints}(X, D, Q)$
    \State $q_1, \dots, q_Q \gets \textsc{VacancySites}(X, D, Q)$

    \State $X^{\text{best}}_{\text{nb}} \gets X$,
    $e^{\text{best}}_{\text{nb}} \gets \infty$,
    $\emph{BestMove} \gets \textsc{Nil}$
    \For{$(i, j) \in [Q] \times [Q]$}
    \State $X_{\text{nb}} \gets
    \textsc{LocalOpt}(\textsc{MovePoint}(X, p_i, q_j), D)$
    \State $a \gets \left(\textsc{Move}(p_i, q_j) \text{ not forbidden}\right)$
    \textbf{or} $\left(E_D(X_{\text{nb}}) < E_D(X^{\text{best}})\right)$
    \If {$a$ \textbf{and} $E_D(X_{\text{nb}}) < e^{\text{best}}_{\text{nb}}$}
    \State $X^{\text{best}}_{\text{nb}} \gets X_{\text{nb}}$,
    $e^{\text{best}}_{\text{nb}} \gets E_D(X_{\text{nb}})$
    \State $\emph{BestMove} \gets \textsc{Move}(p_i, q_j)$
    \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \If{$\emph{BestMove} \ne \textsc{Nil}$}
    \State $X \gets X^{\text{best}}_{\text{nb}}$
    \State Add $\emph{BestMove}$ and its reverse to \emph{TabuList}
    \EndIf
    \State $X \gets \textsc{MBH}(X, D)$ \label{line:mbh}
    \If{$E_D(X) < E_D(X^{\text{best}})$}
    \State $X^{\text{best}} \gets X$

    \State $\emph{NoImprove} \gets 0$
    \Else
    \State $\emph{NoImprove} \gets \emph{NoImprove} + 1$
    \EndIf
    \EndWhile
    \State \Return $X^{\text{best}}$
  \end{algorithmic}
  \label{alg:tabu_search}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:tabu_search} outlines the Tabu Search procedure.
To visualize the effect of Tabu Search, Fig.~\ref{fig:tabu_search}

The dispersion point shown in red is in a high-energy state due to a

\subsection{Adjust Distance}

After the Tabu Search procedure is completed, the resulting
configuration $X$ is a feasible solution, but typically not optimal.
To find the nearest optimal solution $X^*$ for the given
configuration $X$, we employ the Sequential Unconstrained
Minimization Technique (SUMT) \citep{fiacco1964} as proposed by
\citet{lai2024} to convert the original CpDP (as a constrained
optimization problem) into a sequence of unconstrained optimization
problems and solve each until a feasible local minimum solution is
found. Each unconstrained subproblem can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
  \textit{Minimize} \quad \Phi_\mu(X, D) = -D^2 + \mu \times E(X, D)
  \label{eq:phi_mu}
\end{equation}
where $X$ is a candidate solution, $D$ is the radius of the solution
$X$, $\mu$ is the penalty factor to define the unconstrained
optimization problem.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:adjust_distance} is the method to adjust a
feasible configuration from Algorithm~\ref{alg:tabu_search} into a
local optimum. The distance adjustment method will iteratively
minimize Equation~\ref{eq:phi_mu} for strictly increasing values of
$\mu$. The $-D^2$ term in Equation~\ref{eq:phi_mu} is to ensure that
when we minimize this function, we promote maximizing $D$.
Equation~\ref{eq:O_i} contains $(\max\set{0, D - d(c_i, v)})^2$ which
grows quadratically with $D$. The $-D^2$ term thus ensures
Equation~\ref{eq:phi_mu} is not dominated by $E(X, D)$
\citep{lai2024}. The distance adjustment procedure slightly adjusts
the configuration $X$ and the allowed minimum distance $D$ through
local optimization to find a local optimum of the original CpDP.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Adjust Distance}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $X_0$ (configuration), $D_0 \in \R_{> 0}$, $K \in \Z_{> 0}$,
    $\epsilon \in \R_{>0}$, $\mu_{\mathrm{s}} \in \R_{>0}$ (default $1$)
    \State $X \gets X_0$, $D \gets D_0$, $\mu \gets \mu_{\mathrm{s}}$
    \For{$i \gets 1$ to $K$}
    \State $\mu \gets 10 \cdot \mu$
    \State $(X, D) \gets \textsc{LocalOptPhi}(X, D, \mu)$
    \If{$(X, D)$ unchanged for three consecutive iterations}
    \State \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \If{$E_D(X) \ge \epsilon$}
    \Comment{infeasible: snap to the exact bound}
    \State $D \gets \min \set{0.95 D,\ B(X)}$ if $B(X) > 0$, else $0.95 D$
    \State $X \gets \textsc{LocalOpt}(X, D)$
    \State $(X, D) \gets \textsc{AdjustDistance}(X, D, 5, \epsilon, 10^3)$
    \EndIf
    \If{$B(X) > 0$}
    \State $D \gets B(X)$
    \Comment{exactly what $X$ supports}
    \EndIf
    \State \Return $(X, D)$
  \end{algorithmic}
  \label{alg:adjust_distance}
\end{algorithm}

The \textsc{LocalOptPhi} function is a run of L-BFGS-B on the function
$\Phi_\mu$ from an initial solution $(X, D)$ and penalty factor $\mu$. We take
$\mu_0 = 10$ and $\mu \gets 10 \mu$ with $K = 15$, so the schedule spans
$10$ to $10^{15}$. A larger $\mu_0$ makes the first gradient ill-conditioned:
the penalty term then dominates $-D^2$ from the outset, and the first iterate
is driven to satisfy the constraints at whatever radius it starts from rather
than trading radius against violation. Starting small and multiplying is what
lets each iteration begin near the previous minimizer.

Two quantities make the procedure self-correcting. Write
\begin{equation}
  B(X) = \min \set{\min_{i < j} \tfrac{1}{2} d(c_i, c_j),\ \min_i d(c_i,
  \partial C)},
  \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
the largest radius the configuration $X$ admits. Both constraints tighten
monotonically in $D$ (constraint (\textit{ii}) needs $d(c_i, c_j) \ge 2D$
and constraint (\textit{iii}) needs $d(c_i, \partial C) \ge D$), so the
feasible radii for a fixed $X$ are exactly the interval $[0, B(X)]$, and
$B(X)$ is attained. Every $D$ we report is therefore certified feasible by
construction rather than by tolerance.

This matters at both ends of the loop. If SUMT terminates infeasible, $D$ is
lowered in one step to $\min \set{0.95 D, B(X)}$: by at least $5\%$, and
directly to what the configuration supports when that is lower, instead of
being walked down in fixed proportional decrements, which may exhaust their
retries while still infeasible. When a point has left the container, $B(X) =
0$ and only the $5\%$ step is taken. The retry, nested at most five deep,
resumes the penalty schedule at $\mu = 10^4$ rather than restarting it, since
the configuration it continues from is already close to feasible.
And whichever side of $B(X)$ SUMT stops on, the radius returned is $B(X)$
itself. Above it, the tolerance $E < \epsilon$ alone would admit a violation
of up to $\sqrt{\epsilon}$; below it, the radius would be conservative. In
practice $B(X) = 0$ only when a point has left the container, and that case is
reported as infeasible. (We return $B(X) (1 - 10^{-9})$, a margin against
rounding in the final evaluation.) The lower guard is not cosmetic: $D = 0$ is a stationary point of $\Phi_\mu$ at
the lower bound whenever the points are well separated, since $E = 0$ and
$\partial \Phi_\mu / \partial D = -2D$ both vanish there, so an over-estimated
$D_{\text{init}}$ can drive the procedure onto a trivially ``feasible''
answer: in development runs without the bound, every seed of the H-box at
$p = 1$ returned $D = 0$.

Note that \textsc{LocalOpt} is called in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:adjust_distance} only on the infeasible branch, where $D$
has just changed and minimizing $E_D(X)$ can therefore still help. It is not
called in the main loop, where $D$ is free and \textsc{LocalOptPhi} already
varies it. Conversely it would not help to call \textsc{LocalOptPhi} in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:tabu_search}, since Tabu Search is only designed for
finding feasible configurations at a fixed $D$. It is important not to
``rush'' finding a maximal value of $D$, as finding a stable and feasible
configuration $X$ first often leads to a smoother maximization of $D$.

\subsection{Global Optimization}

The global optimization procedure is summarized in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:global_opt}. This algorithm represents a
three-dimensional analogue to the TSGO framework established by
\citet{lai2024}. While \citet{lai2024} utilized a time-bounded loop
with continuous dependency on the global best state, our approach
adopts an iterative multi-start strategy: each restart begins from a fresh
random configuration and shares with the others only the best radius $D^*$
found so far, at which its Tabu Search is run.

By shifting to an iteration-based structure, we ensure that the
algorithm extensively samples the configuration space. Each iteration
begins with a fresh randomization, preventing the solver from
becoming trapped in a previously found local optimum. This is
particularly crucial for non-convex containers where the feasible
region may be disconnected.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Global Optimization}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $p \in \mathbb{N}$, $C$, $D_{\text{init}} \in \R_{>0}$, $N$ (most
    restarts), $R$ (patience), $T$ (time limit), $\epsilon \in \R_{> 0}$
    \Ensure Best configuration $(X^*, D^*)$

    \State $D \gets D_{\text{init}}$
    \State $X \gets \textsc{RandomSolution}(p)$
    \State $X \gets \textsc{TabuSearch}(X, D)$
    \State $X, D \gets \textsc{AdjustDistance}(X, D, 15)$
    \State $X^*, D^* \gets X, D$
    \State $s \gets 0$

    \For {$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
    \If{$s \ge R$ \textbf{or} more than $T$ has elapsed}
    \State \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
    \State $D_i \gets D^*$, \quad $s \gets s + 1$
    \State $X_i \gets \textsc{RandomSolution}(p)$
    \State $X_i \gets \textsc{TabuSearch}(X_i, D_i)$
    \If{$E_{D_i}(X_i) < \epsilon$}
    \State $(X_i, D_i) \gets \textsc{AdjustDistance}(X_i, D_i, 15)$
    \If{$D_i > D^*$ and $E_{D_i}(X_i) < \epsilon$}
    \If{$D_i > (1 + 10^{-7}) D^*$}
    \State $s \gets 0$
    \EndIf
    \State $X^*, D^* \gets X_i, D_i$
    \EndIf
    \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State $(X^*, D^*) \gets \textsc{Polish}(X^*)$
    \State $(X^*, D^*) \gets \textsc{Escape}(X^*, D^*)$ \label{line:escape}
    \Comment{restarts aimed at $1.02\,D^*$; never lowers $D^*$}
    \If{\textsc{Escape} raised $D^*$}
    \State $(X^*, D^*) \gets \textsc{Polish}(X^*)$
    \EndIf
    \State \Return $(X^*, D^*)$
  \end{algorithmic}
  \label{alg:global_opt}
\end{algorithm}

Within each iteration, the algorithm retains the rigorous local
search operators utilized by \citet{lai2024}. An initial
configuration is generated via \textsc{RandomSolution}, followed by
\textsc{TabuSearch} to locate the nearest local minimum. We then
employ a strict feasibility check: only if the feasibility-residual
value $E_D(X)$ for the configuration $X$ falls below the tolerance
threshold $\epsilon$ does the algorithm proceed to
\textsc{AdjustDistance}. This ensures that computational resources in
the expensive refinement phase are reserved solely for valid,
high-quality candidate solutions that have a genuine potential to
improve upon the current global maximum $D^*$. At the local level, the
subroutines minimize Equation~\ref{eq:E} with a gradient-based solver, using
the closed-form gradients described above.

\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{sec:complexity}

Let $n_C$ be the total number of vertices, edges, faces and fan triangles of
the container. For a fixed norm, one evaluation of $E_D$ and its gradient costs
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{O}(p^2 + p \, n_C),
  \label{eq:eval_cost}
\end{equation}
the first term for the pairs of points and the second for each point against
the boundary. As the gradient is expressed in closed form, its order is the
same as the residual, whereas finite differences would need $3p + 1$ evaluations
of $E_D$ per gradient. In our implementation, skipping pairs too far apart to
interact lowers the constant but not this worst-case order.

The \textsc{LocalOpt} of a neighbor move runs at most $L = 100$
iterations of L-BFGS-B, each using a bounded number of evaluations in its line
search, and so costs $\mathcal{O}(L (p^2 + p \, n_C))$. One 
iteration of Algorithm~\ref{alg:tabu_search} performs $Q^2$ of these, which is
$\mathcal{O}(\ln^2 p)$ with $Q = \max(3, \operatorname{round}(\ln p))$, and
then 10 hops of \textsc{MBH}. Scoring the $10p$ vacancy sites and the $p$
points adds only the order of Equation~\ref{eq:eval_cost}. The hops of
\textsc{MBH} and the runs of \textsc{LocalOptPhi} stop on the convergence tests
of the solver rather than on this cap. Algorithm~\ref{alg:adjust_distance}
performs at most 15 penalty solves, and at
most five nested retries of at most 5 solves each, so 40 in all, and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:global_opt} performs at most $N$ restarts, stopping after
$R$ restarts without a gain or once the time limit $T$ has passed;
the first start always runs to completion, and \textsc{Escape}, which has its
own time limit, is excluded from this count.

The problem is NP-hard and $E_D$ is non-convex, so we claim no bound on the
number of iterations needed to reach a global optimum, and Tabu Search
continues for as long as it improves within $\beta$ iterations. We therefore
measure how the total run time grows with $p$ in
Section~\ref{sec:runtime}.

\section{Results and Evaluation}

Every run reported here was made on one core of a compute node of the
Illinois Campus Cluster (two AMD EPYC 7713 processors, 128 cores and 512 GB of
RAM per node), one run per core. CPU times are given in seconds. The algorithm is
implemented in Python 3.11, and the source code is publicly available
(\href{https://github.com/leadcatlab/continuous-p-dispersion}{Source
Code}). The repository's \texttt{supplement} folder lists the centers of every
configuration we report, at full double precision, with each container's
faces and a standalone script, \texttt{verify\_packing.py}, that needs only
NumPy and none of our solver. From the centers alone it recomputes the
largest feasible radius exactly, in any of the three norms: half the smallest
pairwise distance, or the smallest distance from a center to a face, whichever
is less, with every center inside the container. For all 374 configurations it agrees with the radius we report, which sits a relative $10^{-9}$ below it by
design. Our specialized hybrid solver pairs Tabu Search with an
L-BFGS-B optimizer to efficiently handle the problem's $3p+1$ variables
and highly non-convex solution space, avoiding the local minima traps
that stall standard generic solvers. We enforce a constraint
violation tolerance of $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ and truncate reported
results to eight decimal places, ensuring the reported configurations
are strictly feasible within the container geometry.

We evaluate our algorithm on five polyhedral containers of increasing
geometric complexity: the unit cube, unit-length tetrahedron,
H-shaped box, star-shaped polyhedron, and a ribbed ventilation cage.
The boundaries for each are defined using a vectorized representation
of inward-pointing normals for efficient distance calculations. To
evaluate robustness, we benchmarked our algorithm against Packomania
records \citep{packomania}, the results of \citet{kazakov2018}, optimality
certificates from Gurobi \citep{gurobi2026}, and two general-purpose nonlinear
programming solvers, IPOPT \citep{wachter2006} and SLSQP \citep{kraft1988,
scipy2020}, run under an equal time budget. The protocol for that comparison
(solvers, formulations, cells, budgets and statistics) was fixed in writing
before any run, and is given in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}.

Every run is Algorithm~\ref{alg:global_opt}: a first start from
$D_{\text{init}}$, then restarts from fresh random configurations at the best
radius found so far, until $R = \lceil 3 \ln p \rceil$ restarts in a row fail
to raise it by more than a relative $10^{-7}$, and at most $N = 40$ restarts
and $T = 30$ minutes per run; a restart under way when the time runs out
stops there, and its configuration counts only if it is feasible. The first
start always runs to completion, since the run has no answer before it ends.
Every run then ends with the escape phase described below, which has a limit
of its own. Tabu Search depth is $\beta = 10$ and the tabu-rearrangement
parameter is $Q = \max(3, \operatorname{round}(\ln p))$, which is the value
$Q = 3$ of \citet{lai2024} for $p \le 33$. Each cell (container, metric, $p$)
is run from $20$ independent seeds. The tables report the best of them; the
mean, median and standard deviation of the radius and the run time for every
cell are in the repository (\texttt{supplement/tables/summary\_stats.csv}).
In 169 of the 240 cells all 20 seeds reach the best radius, to a relative
$10^{-6}$; in 28 only one does, all but one of them at $p \ge 20$, where runs
reach the time limits (Section~\ref{sec:runtime}). The restarts
are what put Tabu Search to work. From a start at or below $\hat D$ (Equation~\ref{eq:dinit_of_rho}), the first
\textsc{LocalOpt} usually reaches $E_D < \epsilon$ on its own, and Tabu Search
then performs no iterations at all; a restart at the best radius so far rarely
does, so there the search has to relocate points to find a feasible
configuration. The three settings justified below, the restarts, their
stopping rule and $Q$, were chosen in development experiments on an earlier
version of the solver, without the escape phase; we report those experiments
as the basis for the choices, not as results of the final code. In a pilot on
12 cells spanning all five containers and three metrics, with $25$ starts per
cell either way, four restarts per run (the design then in use) found a larger
radius than one start per run in six cells, by $1.0\%$ to $6.7\%$, was behind in none by more than $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ (relative), and
used a third of the CPU time. How many restarts to make we settled by
replaying 160 long runs (cube, tetrahedron, H-box and star; $\ell_2$ and
$\ell_1$; $p = 10$ to $100$; capped at 41 starts or 30 minutes) against
stopping rules constant in $p$ and growing as $\ln p$, $\sqrt p$ and $p$:
$\lceil 3 \ln p \rceil$ used $81\%$ of the capped CPU time, lost $0.003\%$ of
the radius on average, and lost more than $0.01\%$ in one cell of 40. At large
$p$ the time limit binds first (Section~\ref{sec:runtime}). $Q$ grows with $p$
because, at a fixed budget, $Q = 5$ beat $3$ and $6$ on the cube at $p = 100$,
and $Q = 4$ beat $3$ in 12 of 24 paired runs, and lost in 5, on the tetrahedron and H-box at $p = 60$.

\textsc{Polish} tightens the run's best configuration by sequential linear
programming. Every distance in the constraints is a convex function of the
points, so its linearization at the current configuration is a lower bound
on it, and a configuration that satisfies the linearized constraints
satisfies the original ones. Each step solves that linear program, for the
points and the radius, inside a trust region; its result is certified
exactly and kept only if the radius rises, so the polish never lowers it.
It closes the gap of a few parts in $10^5$ by which the penalty method stops
short of a configuration's local maximum, and with it the cube at $p = 100$
reaches the Packomania record. These values are recorded in every row of our
results table.

\paragraph{Escape phase} A restart runs Tabu Search at the
best radius found so far, and Tabu Search moves points only while the
residual is positive. A random start that is already feasible at that radius
is therefore returned unmoved, however poor its layout, and a run whose best
configuration is held in place by a narrow feature of the container can
repeat it on every restart. Every run therefore ends with an \emph{escape phase}: once the restarts
stop, further restarts run Tabu Search at a target $2\%$ above the best radius, where such a
start is infeasible and has to move. Each grows its radius from wherever Tabu
Search leaves it and replaces the best only if it beats it, so the phase can
only raise a run's result. It stops after as many restarts without a gain as
the main loop allows, or after 15 minutes, a start under way stopping at the
limit; these are the only settings we tried.

We designed this phase after seeing runs held in place in three cells: example
6 of \citet{stoyan2013}, the H-box under $\ell_\infty$ at $p = 56$, and the
ribbed cage under $\ell_2$ at $p = 100$. Those cells therefore informed its
design and are not independent evidence for it. It is part of every run, and
every table and figure reports runs made with it.

The initial radius is estimated by filling a fraction $\rho_{\text{init}} =
0.25$ of the container with $p$ balls \citep{groemer1986, lai2024}, then
capping at the container's inradius:
\begin{equation}
  \hat D = \min \set{\sqrt[3]{\frac{\rho_{\text{init}} \,
  V_{\text{container}}}{\kappa_d \, p}},\ r_{\text{in}}(C)}.
  \label{eq:dinit_of_rho}
\end{equation}
Here $\kappa_d$ is the volume of the unit ball of the metric $d$, so that a
ball of radius $D$ occupies $\kappa_d D^3$: it is $\tfrac43\pi$ for $\ell_2$,
$8$ for $\ell_\infty$, whose ball is a cube of side $2D$, and $\tfrac43$ for
$\ell_1$, whose ball is a cross-polytope. The metric belongs in the estimate
because the heuristic is a statement about volume, and the three balls differ
in volume by a factor of six at the same radius. Using the Euclidean constant
throughout, as is tempting, over-estimates $\hat D$ by $1.24\times$ under the
sup norm, with consequences described below.
The cap is not a refinement but a necessity. A volume-based estimate knows
nothing about shape, and on containers whose volume is distributed into thin
or branching regions it over-estimates badly: by $1.5\times$ on the H-box
and $1.9\times$ on the cage. Since no $D$ above the inradius is feasible at
any $p$, an uncapped estimate starts the search outside the feasible range of
radii entirely, which is the regime in which $D = 0$ becomes an attracting
stationary point of $\Phi_\mu$; the cap and the bound $B(X)$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:bound} together keep the search out of it.
The same expression is used by both the solver and the experiment driver, so
the two cannot disagree.

$\hat D$ is an estimate, and neither direction of error is free. Starting
above the achievable radius sends Algorithm~\ref{alg:adjust_distance} into its
infeasible branch: $D$ is snapped to $B(X)$ for whatever configuration it is
then holding, and the search continues from that configuration rather than
from a fresh one. Starting below it, $\Phi_\mu$ drives $D$ upward, but Tabu
Search has by then settled the points into an arrangement sized for the
smaller radius, and that arrangement can confine the climb. In development
runs of an earlier version of the solver, both failures occurred on the unit
cube under the sup norm: a high start ended at $0.2299$ rather than the
optimum $\tfrac14$ at $p = 2$, and five of 12 low starts ended at $\tfrac18$
rather than $\tfrac16$ at $p = 20$. With the ladder and the escape phase,
all 20 seeds reach the optimum in both cells.

We therefore treat the initial radius as a second multi-start dimension. A
cell's seeds are distributed over a geometric ladder of $M$ rungs spanning a
factor $\lambda$ either side of the estimate,
\begin{equation}
  D_{\text{init}}^{(k)} = \lambda^{\frac{2k}{M - 1} - 1} \, \hat D,
  \qquad k = 0, \dots, M - 1,
  \label{eq:dinit_ladder}
\end{equation}
each clipped at $r_{\text{in}}(C)$, with $M = 5$ and $\lambda = 1.7$. Seed $s$
starts on rung $s \bmod M$, so each rung receives four of a cell's 20 seeds.
The spread is set by measurement rather than tuned: across the non-Euclidean
cells the best radius found lies between $0.77 \hat D$ and $1.59 \hat D$, so
$\lambda = 1.7$ brackets the observed range with margin. Since every reported
radius is already the best over a cell's seeds, this costs no additional runs;
it widens the multi-start from configurations alone to pairs of
configuration and initial radius. The inradius used for the cap is computed
numerically, by maximizing clearance from the best of $64$ sampled interior
points, and so can only err low; it is a conservative cap.

A start high on the ladder can end infeasible, and such a run is not
discarded. It is restarted from the same seed at a lower radius, half a rung
at a time: a run that fails from $D_{\text{init}}^{(k)}$ is restarted from
$\tfrac12 \paren{D_{\text{init}}^{(k-1)} + D_{\text{init}}^{(k)}}$, one that
fails from there is restarted from $D_{\text{init}}^{(k-1)}$, and so on down to
$D_{\text{init}}^{(0)}$. Only a run still infeasible from
$D_{\text{init}}^{(0)}$ is reported infeasible. Because a restart keeps its
seed, it begins from the same random configuration and differs from the failed
attempt only in its radius; steps that the inradius cap merges are taken once,
since repeating a radius would repeat the failure. Every reported time
includes all of a run's attempts. In the sweep reported here the fallback was
needed by $36$ of $4{,}800$ runs: 33 succeeded half a rung down and three after
two steps, and no run ended infeasible.

The ladder is not arbitrary: the optimum is bracketed by two bounds that hold
for any norm and any container, convex or not. Write $V = \operatorname{volume}(C)$
and
\begin{equation}
  C_{-D} = \set{x \in C : d(x, \partial C) \ge D}
  \label{eq:inner_body}
\end{equation}
for the set of admissible centers at radius $D$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bracket}
  Let $D^*(p)$ be the optimal radius for $p$ points in $C$ under a norm
  whose unit ball has volume $\kappa_d$. Then
  \begin{equation*}
    \underline{D}(p) \;\le\; D^*(p) \;\le\;
    \min \set{\paren{\frac{V}{\kappa_d \, p}}^{1/3},\ r_{\text{in}}(C)},
  \end{equation*}
  where $\underline{D}(p) = \sup \set{D > 0 : \operatorname{volume}(C_{-D}) >
  8 \kappa_d (p - 1) D^3}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  The balls of radius $D$ about the points of any feasible configuration lie
  in $C$, by constraint (\textit{iii}), and have pairwise disjoint interiors,
  since $d(c_i, c_j) \ge 2D$ and the triangle inequality forbids a common
  interior point. Their volumes therefore sum to at most $V$, so
  $p \kappa_d D^3 \le V$. And $D \le d(c_1, \partial C) \le r_{\text{in}}(C)$.
  Together these give the upper bound.

  For the lower bound, fix $D$ with $\operatorname{volume}(C_{-D}) > 8 \kappa_d
  (p - 1) D^3$. By the argument just given, any subset of $C_{-D}$ whose
  points are pairwise at least $2D$ apart has at most $V / (\kappa_d D^3)$
  elements, so there is such a subset $Y$ of largest size. Every $x \in
  C_{-D}$ lies within distance $2D$ of some $y \in Y$, as otherwise $Y \cup
  \set{x}$ would be larger. Hence $C_{-D}$ is covered by the balls of radius
  $2D$ about the points of $Y$, and $\operatorname{volume}(C_{-D}) \le |Y| \,
  \kappa_d (2D)^3$. By the choice of $D$ this forces $|Y| > p - 1$. Any $p$
  points of $Y$ are then a feasible configuration at radius $D$: they lie in
  $C_{-D}$, which is constraint (\textit{iii}), and are pairwise $2D$ apart,
  which is constraint (\textit{ii}). So $D^*(p) \ge D$, and taking the
  supremum over such $D$ gives the lower bound.
\end{proof}

Nothing in the proof uses convexity, the shape of the ball beyond its volume,
or the dimension beyond the exponent. With the cap inactive, the upper bound
is $\rho_{\text{init}}^{-1/3} \hat D = 1.587 \hat D$, so the top rung, at
$1.7 \hat D$, starts above the optimum in every cell: it is on the fallback
that it depends. On the two convex containers, where $C_{-D}$ is an
intersection of half-spaces and its volume is exact, $\underline{D}(p)$ lies
between $0.51 \hat D$ and $0.71 \hat D$ for $2 \le p \le 84$ in all three
metrics, against best radii found between $0.80 \hat D$ and $1.59 \hat D$.
The top of that range is the upper bound itself: under the sup norm, eight
cubes of side $\tfrac12$ tile the unit cube, and the $p = 8$ radius
$\tfrac14$ is exactly $\rho_{\text{init}}^{-1/3} \hat D$.
The lower bound is what the covering argument can certify, not what a search
reaches: a start at $\underline{D}(p)$ is guaranteed to admit a feasible
configuration, but lies below the ladder's second rung ($0.77 \hat D$), among
the low starts that missed the sup-norm optimum in the development runs
described above. On the non-convex containers the bound
needs a lower estimate of $\operatorname{volume}(C_{-D})$, which a decomposition
into convex pieces with disjoint interiors supplies, since their inner bodies
are disjoint subsets of $C_{-D}$; we have not evaluated it there.

The effect is confined to the non-Euclidean metrics, and is not small. In
development runs of an earlier version of the solver that used the Euclidean
constant throughout, the radii violated monotonicity in $p$ ($D$ rising as
points were added, which is impossible, since a configuration of $p + 1$
points contains one of $p$) in 19 cells, every one of them sup norm and none
of them $\ell_1$, exactly the asymmetry the constants $\kappa_d$ predict.
With the constants of the metric, none of the 240 cells of the main
experiment violates it.

\subsection{H-Box}\label{sec:hbox}

Non-convex containers, such as the H-shaped polyhedron, present
significant geometric challenges for the CpDP. Unlike convex domains,
non-convex shapes often feature disconnected feasible regions and
obstructed lines of sight, where the straight-line segment connecting
two valid points may pass through the infeasible exterior
\citep{dai2021}. These characteristics create numerous ``trapped''
local minima that can stall standard optimization algorithms
\citep{lai2024}. The H-shaped polyhedron was created with inspiration
from its two-dimensional analogue: the H-shaped polygon \citep{dai2021}.

Table~\ref{tab:h_box} reports the best radius found for each $p$
in all three metrics. No published values exist for this container; the
comparison with general-purpose solvers is in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}, where
the H-box is one of the containers on which they fall behind.

\input{generated/results_h_box}

Under the sup norm each ball is an axis-aligned cube of side $2D$, and at
$p = 56$ the H-box admits an exact tiling: 56 cubes of side $\tfrac12$ have total
volume $56 \cdot \tfrac18 = 7$, the volume of the container. The radius
$D = \tfrac14$ therefore meets the upper bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:bracket},
$(V / (\kappa_\infty p))^{1/3} = (7 / (8 \cdot 56))^{1/3} = \tfrac14$, and is
optimal. All 20 of our runs find it, at $\tfrac14$ to within the relative
$10^{-9}$ by which every reported radius is shrunk (Fig.~\ref{fig:h_box_sup_56}).
The tiling needs every cube on the grid at once. The monotone basin hopping
inside Tabu Search cannot make that rearrangement, and a restart at a smaller
radius such as $\tfrac3{14}$ or $\tfrac16$ is feasible at once, so Tabu Search
alone leaves it unmoved: in 60 runs of an earlier version of the solver,
without the escape phase, none found the tiling; 24 settled on
$D = \tfrac3{14}$ and 36 on $D = \tfrac16$, at $86\%$ and $67\%$ of the
optimum. The escape phase aims above the best radius, where such a restart is
infeasible and has to move. This cell is outside the grid of
Table~\ref{tab:h_box}.

\subsection{Star-Shaped Polyhedron}

The star-shaped polyhedron draws inspiration from the Kepler--Poinsot
polyhedron \citep{coxeter1989} and serves as a rigorous test case for
non-convex optimization due to its distinctive central convex core
connected to six narrow, tapering spikes. This originally developed
geometry creates significant ``bottlenecks'' between the feasible
sub-regions, making it difficult for optimization algorithms to
migrate points from one spike to another. Unlike convex containers
where local descent directions are often globally informative, the
star-shaped polyhedron requires an algorithm capable of traversing
infeasible space to escape the local optima found within individual spikes.

In Table~\ref{tab:pointy_star}, we report the best radius $D$ found by our
algorithm for a range of $p$ values, up to $p = 100$.

\input{generated/results_pointy_star}

The results indicate that our algorithm effectively exploits the
available volume even as the number of points increases. For $p=1$,
the algorithm identifies the globally optimal placement within the
central core. As $p$ increases to 6, the algorithm successfully
distributes points into the six available spikes, maintaining a high
separation distance of $D \approx 0.9030$. From $p = 7$ on, crowding
effects force a gradual reduction in the minimum distance $D$ as the
central core and spikes become filled.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ps_100} shows the best configuration found for $p = 100$. Only
24 of the 100 balls sit in the central core; the other 76 are split almost
evenly among the six spikes, 12 or 13 in each, and the outermost centers lie
3.5 of the 5 units from the center of the star to a tip. The algorithm does
not crowd the core and leave the spikes empty: it carries points through the
bottlenecks and fills every spike to a similar depth.

\subsection{Ribbed Ventilation Cage}

Finally, we apply our algorithm to the ``ribbed ventilation cage,'' a
highly complex non-convex polyhedron designed to model modern
industrial computer casings and heat sinks. Unlike the H-shaped box
or Star-shaped polyhedron, which feature large distinct sub-regions,
this container is characterized by numerous corridors and separated
legs. The inclusion of numerous internal pillars and ribs creates a
``maze-like'' feasible region, introducing frequent discontinuities
that pose severe challenges for traditional gradient-based nonlinear optimizers.

The particular design of this ribbed ventilation cage polyhedral
container is original, although the complexity is practically
motivated. In thermal engineering, components utilize ribbed
structures, fins, and microstructures to maximize surface area and
improve heat dissipation capabilities \citep{xin2025}. Solving the
CpDP on such a geometry is therefore relevant for optimizing the
layout of sensors, cooling pipes, or modular components within
densely packed electronic enclosures. We evaluate our algorithm using
both the Euclidean metric ($\ell_2$), representing standard spherical
component packing, and the sup norm ($\ell_\infty$), which is
critical for modeling the placement of cubic or box-shaped components
often found in hardware design.

metrics.

\subsection{Comparison with General-Purpose Solvers}\label{sec:baselines}

A specialized method should be tested against general-purpose solvers handed
the same problem. We compare under a protocol written before the first run of
this comparison, which commits us to reporting every cell, including those we
lose. Its deviations are listed, each dated, with the protocol in the
repository. The last of them was made after earlier runs of the comparison:
our algorithm includes the escape phase, the budgets come from the main
experiment reported here, and every trial ran on the same cluster.

\paragraph{Solvers and formulation} The baselines are IPOPT 3.14, an
interior-point method \citep{wachter2006}, and SLSQP, sequential quadratic
programming \citep{kraft1988, scipy2020}. Each is given the constrained program
$\max D$ subject to $d(c_i, c_j) \ge 2D$ and a clearance of at least $D$ for
every point, formulated as a practitioner would pose it. Dispersion is
$\norm{c_i - c_j}_2^2 \ge 4D^2$ under $\ell_2$; under $\ell_1$ and
$\ell_\infty$, whose norms are not differentiable, it uses a smooth
approximation of width $10^{-6}$ times the container's extent. On the convex
containers, containment is one exact linear constraint per face,
$\ang{n_f, c_i - P_f} \ge D \norm{n_f}_*$. On the non-convex ones it is a signed
clearance oracle, the exact distance to the boundary inside and its negative
outside, with an analytic gradient, so that a solver always has a direction
back into the container. Both solvers receive analytic Jacobians, are
restarted from random configurations until their budget ends, and never see
our energy model or search.

\paragraph{Budget and cells} The 45 cells are every container and metric at
$p \in \set{10, 20, 40}$. Each solver makes 10 trials per cell, one core each,
and every trial on a cell receives the same wall time,
$\max \set{60\,\text{s},\ 2 t_{\text{med}}}$, where $t_{\text{med}}$ is our
algorithm's median run time on that cell in the main experiment. Our algorithm runs as in the main experiment and then continues its escape
phase until the budget ends. A start or local solve still running when the
budget ends does not count. Every radius,
for every solver, is scored as certified by the bound $B(X)$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:bound}.

\paragraph{Quality} At equal time our algorithm finds a larger radius
(best of 10) than IPOPT in 30 of the 45 cells and than SLSQP in 23, and ties
the rest to within $10^{-6}$ (relative); it is behind in none. A
two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired best radii gives
$p = 5.7 \times 10^{-14}$ against IPOPT and $p = 9.0 \times 10^{-4}$ against
SLSQP. The advantage follows the geometry
(Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_quality}): on the convex containers at $p = 10$ all
three solvers reach the same radius, and our margin there is at most $6.8\%$
at $p = 20$ and $40$, while on the non-convex containers it is ahead by
up to $2.4\times$ IPOPT and $1.6\times$ SLSQP (both on the cage, $\ell_1$,
$p = 40$). In every non-convex container the largest gap is at
$p = 40$.

\paragraph{Speed} The general-purpose solvers are faster where they succeed.
In the 12 cells in which the median trial of all three solvers reaches
$99.9\%$ of the best radius found, ten of them convex, SLSQP gets there sooner
than our algorithm in seven, by up to $17\times$, and IPOPT in five, by up to
$2.5\times$; our algorithm is first in the other five. Our algorithm pays for its
ladder start and Tabu Search before its first result: a trial whose first start
is still running when the budget ends scores zero, which happened in two of its 450
trials, both started at the top of the ladder; the best of 10 in those cells is unaffected. Its
advantage is reach, not speed. In 16 cells only our algorithm reaches the
target in most trials; the reverse never happens, and in 8 cells no solver
does. Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_profile} puts both facts in one picture. SLSQP is
the first to the target in 8 cells and reaches it in 20, 12 of them after our
algorithm: when it succeeds it is fast, and otherwise it stalls below the
target. Our algorithm is first in 29 cells and reaches the target later in 8
more, 37 in all; IPOPT reaches it in 13 and is never first. Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_anytime} shows the
same pattern over time on four hard cells: the baselines stall below the best
known radius. On the star, the H-box and the cube our algorithm reaches it
within the first few hundred seconds; on the cage it starts later, after its
first start completes, and keeps improving.

\paragraph{Feasibility of reported radii} A local solver stops when its
constraint violation falls below a tolerance, so the radius it reports can
exceed the radius its configuration supports. Of SLSQP's converged local
solves, $41.8\%$ over-state the radius by more than $10^{-6}$ (relative),
$1.0\%$ by more than $1\%$, and $1.1\%$ report convergence on a configuration
that supports no positive radius at all. For IPOPT the shares are $17.4\%$ and
under $0.01\%$. Every radius in this comparison, for every solver, is therefore
the certified $B(X)$; our algorithm reports $B(X)$ by construction.

\paragraph{Optimality} For the convex containers under $\ell_2$, Gurobi's
spatial branch-and-bound \citep{gurobi2026}, warm-started from our best
configuration, closes its optimality gap within one hour for the cube with
$p \le 9$ and the tetrahedron with $p \le 10$. In every one of these cases our
radius is within $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ of Gurobi's bound, which is the
resolution of Gurobi's constraint tolerance. For the cube at $p = 10$, where
Gurobi does not close its gap in an hour, Packomania records a proven optimum
of $3/14$, which our radius matches to the relative $10^{-9}$ by which every
reported radius is shrunk. On the tetrahedron at $p = 10$ the proven optimum
is $0.112372$; the benchmark of \citet{kazakov2018}, $0.102$, is $9.2\%$ below
it.

Gurobi also entered the equal-budget comparison on the six cells it can model
(cube and tetrahedron, $\ell_2$, $p = 10, 20, 40$), on one core per trial like
the other solvers, scored by the certified radius of its best incumbent. It
ties our algorithm at $p = 10$ and is behind elsewhere: by $0.7\%$ and $3.1\%$ on
the cube at $p = 20$ and $40$, and by $7.0\%$ and $3.9\%$ on the tetrahedron; at $p = 20$ our algorithm reaches the radius
of the stacked tetrahedral arrangement, $1/(6 + 2\sqrt6)$.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency and Scalability}\label{sec:runtime}

To evaluate scalability, we recorded the wall-clock time of every run, all
of its starts and any fallback attempts included ($\epsilon = 10^{-8}$).
Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_runtime} plots, for every container and $p$, the mean
over the 20 runs of a cell on logarithmic axes, where a power law is a
straight line and its exponent is the slope.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/runtime_euclidean.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Mean solve time per run, Euclidean metric.} Each point is
    the mean wall-clock time over the seeds of one cell; both axes are
    logarithmic. The curves flatten where runs reach the time limits; see
  the text.}
  \label{fig:combined_runtime}
\end{figure}

The time limits shape the right of the plot: 30 minutes for the restarts,
then up to 15 for the escape phase, each stopping a start under way.
1{,}098 of the $4{,}800$ runs took longer than 30 minutes in all, over half of
them at $p \ge 50$, so the mean times there measure the limits as much as the
algorithm, and the curves of the cube, H-box and star flatten as they reach
them, by $p = 50$, $50$ and $30$. We therefore fit $\log t$ against
$\log p$ only over the cells with $p \ge 2$ below the limits, those in which
fewer than $10\%$ of runs pass 30 minutes. This gives exponents of $1.15$ for the
cube ($p \le 40$, $R^2 = 0.94$), $1.78$ for the H-box ($p \le 30$, except $20$, $R^2 = 0.97$),
$1.30$ for the star ($p \le 10$, $R^2 = 0.97$), $1.71$ for the ribbed cage
($p \le 9$, $R^2 = 0.98$) and $0.94$ for the tetrahedron (every $p$ except $40$,
$R^2 = 0.97$), all below 2, the exponent of the worst-case cost of one evaluation
(Equation~\ref{eq:eval_cost}), which skipping distant pairs keeps from
being reached at these sizes; the short ranges of the star and the cage make theirs the least
certain. On the tetrahedron the time is set less by $p$ than by how
soon the restarts stop raising the radius, which ends a run after
$\lceil 3 \ln p\rceil$ restarts without a gain: the mean is 483 to 1{,}027
seconds at the tetrahedral numbers $p = 35, 56$ and $84$, and 2{,}260 seconds
at $p = 40$. On the cage the cost is set mostly by feasibility recovery inside
a domain whose free volume is broken into narrow channels; its mean is about
2{,}500 seconds from $p = 20$ on, except $1{,}884$ at $p = 50$.

The slowest cell, the cage under $\ell_\infty$ at $p = 100$, averages $4{,}583$ seconds per run:
the first start, which always runs to completion, takes most of it on the
seeds that start high on the ladder.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduces a robust global optimization framework for the
Continuous $p$-Dispersion Problem (CpDP) in three-dimensional
polyhedral containers under any norm-induced metric. By defining an
almost-everywhere differentiable
feasibility-residual function and extending the Tabu Search algorithm
\cite{lai2024}, we successfully transition from restrictive
two-dimensional planar checks to a comprehensive three-dimensional
formulation utilizing an exact signed-tetrahedra inclusion test and projection-based
distance calculations to faces, edges, and vertices. Empirical
evaluations confirm that our method matches the proven optima and
best-known values in the unit cube \cite{schaer1966, schaer1994, packomania}; reaches
the optima Gurobi certifies in the regular tetrahedron, which the
grid-based values of \citet{kazakov2018} miss by up to $9.2\%$; packs more
spheres than published in seven of the eight multi-connected domains of
\citet{stoyan2013}; and, at equal wall time, finds a smaller radius than
IPOPT or SLSQP in none of 45 cells. It also sets benchmarks for three
non-convex containers of our own design, with every configuration's centers
published alongside a script that checks them, offering a powerful tool for
real-world applications such as robotic
collision avoidance and facility layout \cite{abdulghafoor2021, loganathan2024}.

Restarting at the best radius has a blind spot: a random start already
feasible at that radius is never moved. The escape phase
(line~\ref{line:escape} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:global_opt}) aims its restarts
above the best radius to close it.
In the three cells where an earlier version of the solver was held in place,
no run now ends there, but under $\ell_\infty$ the cage's trap at
$D = \tfrac12$ persists. How far above the best radius the escape phase's
target should sit, and how long it should run, we have not tuned.

Because our energy formulation relies on dimension-independent vector
operations, it provides a natural foundation for extension into
$n$-dimensional spaces ($\mathbb{R}^n$). The primary adaptation
required for higher dimensions is generalizing the geometric
container representation, specifically determining how to precisely
model $(n-1)$-dimensional bounding hyperplanes and extending the
inclusion test, whose signed tetrahedra generalize to signed simplices in
$\R^n$. Furthermore, future work will focus on
incorporating more sophisticated, non-planar geometries such as
curved surfaces. While polyhedral distances currently admit closed
forms (orthogonal projection in the Euclidean case, and a finite
search over the dual unit ball's vertices for a general polyhedral norm),
curved boundaries require
representations like spline patches or implicit surfaces,
necessitating the development of computationally efficient distance
evaluation methods that do not rely on closed-form projections.
